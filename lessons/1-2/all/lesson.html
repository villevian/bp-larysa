🔊 1.01

Marek: Welcome back to the podcast! I'm Marek, and today we are live in Barcelona with Alicia. Alicia, you are an incredible events manager. Tell us, what do you actually do? Do you plan weddings?

Alicia: Haha, no, I don't! Weddings are harder than business lunches. Wedding planners do weddings. I only plan events for companies.

Marek: Ah, corporate life! So who are your main clients?

Alicia: About ninety percent of my corporate clients are businesses — tech companies, banks, and start-ups. I help them plan an event, like a big annual conference, a product launch, or a fun team offsite.

Marek: Sounds busy! And how does it work in 2026? Everything is digital now, right?

Alicia: Totally. Most events now are a hybrid event — you have people in the room and people online. We use QR-code registration at the door, an AI tool for the schedule, and we are streaming the talks live. Plus, we always make sure we have a lot of vegan and gluten-free food for every guest.

Marek: Wow, high-tech! And what about money? How do you manage a budget?

Alicia: Well, we talk about the budget at the first meeting. Prices are higher than they were five years ago. So it's tricky. I can't choose the most expensive hotel in Barcelona for a client with a small budget.

Marek: Makes sense! And what about the opposite?

Alicia: Exactly. For a cheap event, we choose the least expensive venue possible. Speaking of which, I try to find the best location for each event.

Marek: Like that big project you did recently? Tell us about it!

Alicia: Oh, yes! My favorite recent project was a tenth anniversary party for a software company. We rented a fantastic rooftop, invited about three hundred guests, hired a cool DJ for entertainment, and made a strict "no laptops" rule!

Marek: No laptops at a tech party? Brilliant! Haha. But how do you handle everything? You have to arrange all the food, tables and chairs, coordinate the staff, and check every single thing.

Alicia: Oh, absolutely. It's all details! You have to be organised and pay attention to detail.

Marek: And what was the hardest part of that rooftop party? Was it more difficult than your usual events?

Alicia: Actually, yes! Planning an event outdoors is always the biggest challenge. Because of the wind and the unpredictable weather!

Marek: I can imagine. Now, what about the guests after the conference? Do they just fly home?

Alicia: Not anymore! About half of the guests stay for the weekend after the conference. It's huge right now — it's called bleisure — "business and leisure".

Marek: I love that word. Bleisure in Barcelona! Before we finish, Alicia, what skills does an events manager need most? Are any of the skills more important, or less important?

Alicia: Well, you need to get on with people, be totally organised, and have great attention to detail. But the top priority is client satisfaction. Honestly, you need all the skills together!

Marek: Perfect summary. Alicia, thank you so much!

Alicia: De nada, Marek! See you at the next venue!

Types of events: events for companies (not weddings): conferences, product launches, team offsites, hybrid events, a company anniversary party
Important things: the budget, the location / venue, entertainment (a DJ), food (vegan, gluten-free), food, tables and chairs, staff, the weather (outdoor events)
Skills: get on with people, be organised, pay attention to detail; the top priority is client satisfaction
New in 2026: hybrid events, QR-code registration, an AI tool for the schedule, streaming the talks live, higher prices, guests stay for the weekend (bleisure)

B Complete the sentences with comparative, superlative or irregular.

1. To form the of most adjectives, we use the before the adjective and -est or most / least.
2. To form the of most adjectives, we use -er or more / less and than.
3. Some common adjectives are , e.g. good – better than – the best.

Pronunciation bank: the letter r

In British English, we pronounce r before a vowel (restaurant, price), but not at the end of a word or before a consonant (cheaper, park, worst). In American English, we always pronounce r. Listen and compare. Then repeat the British version.
